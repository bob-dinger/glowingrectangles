The Parties in Our Heads

Americans were asked what share of each party belongs to groups associated with it. Reality ranges from 2% to 36%. The guesses barely move. People aren't estimating a quantity — they're reporting how central the group feels to the brand.

Ahler & Sood, The Parties in Our Heads, Journal of Politics 80(3), 2018. YouGov, March 2015, n = 1,000. DATA — measured, not modelled.

What the shape shows

Sorted by reality, the true values climb steadily from 2% to 36% while the guesses sit in a band around 38% the whole way. Perception is nearly flat; only reality varies. Measured as spread, the true figures vary 2.7 times as much as the perceptions of them.

That reframes the finding. It isn't that people overestimate by some amount — it's that the answer barely depends on the question. Asked how many Republicans earn over $250,000 (really 2.2%) and how many are Southern (really 35.7%), people say 38% and 40%. Sixteen-fold difference in fact, two points in belief.

The authors tested the obvious alternative explanations — expressive responding, bad arithmetic, ignorance of base rates — and rejected all three. And the people who follow political news most closely held the most distorted beliefs on seven of the eight groups. The single exception was Republicans earning over $250k, where media coverage of "the 1%" happened to supply the correction.

The title is Walter Lippmann's. Public Opinion (1922) called them "the pictures in our heads."
