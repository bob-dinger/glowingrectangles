A Circus, Not a Census

Somebody says the internet is furious. A reporter writes that X users reacted
with outrage. A cable segment puts three screenshots on the wall and calls it
the national mood.

Here is what was actually measured.

[VISUAL 1 — 500 Americans]

Stand 500 Americans in a room. In 2021, 115 of them had a Twitter account. Pew
got a thousand-odd users to hand over their real handles and then watched what
those accounts did for three months.

The top quarter of them — 29 people out of the 500 — produced 97% of the tweets.

The other 86 posted a median of zero times a month. Not "less." Zero. More than
half the people who had an account said nothing at all.

So when you read the room, you are not reading 500 people. You are not even
reading 115. You are reading 29. About six percent of the adults in the room
are writing essentially all of it, and the other 471 are furniture.

That is the first problem, and it is the small one.


The 29 are not a random 29

If you pulled 29 names out of a hat you would have a sample. A small one, but an
honest one — it would look like the room, give or take.

Nobody pulled these 29 out of a hat. They selected themselves, and then an
algorithm selected again among them, and both filters push the same way.

Pew also compared Twitter users against the general public:

  Median age 40, against 47 for the country.
  42% hold a bachelor's degree, against 31%.
  41% make over $75,000, against 32%.
  60% lean Democratic, against 52%.
  14% call themselves very conservative, against 25% of the public.

Younger, richer, more educated, further left. And that is the account holders —
all 115 of them. The 29 who actually post are a second selection out of that
group, sorted on the single trait of wanting to post.

A census tries to count everybody and is judged on who it missed. A circus sells
tickets and is judged on the show. One of them is measuring the country. The
other is casting it.


Most of it isn't even an opinion

Here is the part I find funniest. Of the tweets that most-active quarter sent:

  49% were retweets.
  33% were replies.
  14% were original.

Run that against the whole pile and roughly one tweet in seven is somebody's own
sentence. The rest is people forwarding each other, and arguing in the margins
of a forward.

So "people are saying" resolves to: about six percent of adults, who are not
much like the other ninety-four, mostly repeating each other.


Why this keeps working

It works because the sample arrives pre-formatted as a quote.

A poll hands you a number and a margin of error. It is boring, and it admits
what it doesn't know. A tweet hands you a human being saying a thing, with a
face and a name and a reply count. It reads as evidence. It is an anecdote
wearing evidence's clothes.

And it's free. A poll costs money and takes a week. A screenshot takes eleven
seconds. The economics guarantee the circus will keep getting reported as the
census, because the census is expensive and the circus is already on your phone.


What it's actually good for

I'm not saying the circus is worthless. Circuses are great. Some of the most
interesting sentences I read this year were on that website, and I don't think a
representative sample of Americans would have produced a single one of them.
That is the whole value: it is not a random draw. You get the obsessives, the
specialists, the people who have been thinking about one strange thing for nine
years and finally have somewhere to put it.

You just can't read the crowd and call it the country.

A habit worth building: when you catch yourself thinking everyone believes X
now, ask how many actual people "everyone" is. You can usually count them. It
is usually about six percent of a room, retweeting each other.
